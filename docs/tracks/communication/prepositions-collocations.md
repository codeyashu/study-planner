# Prepositions & collocations

!!! abstract "At a glance"
    **Priority:** P0 · **Complexity:** 3/5 · **Est. time:** 2 h · **Phase:** 1 · **Prereqs:** none (see also [collocations & precision](collocations-precision.md), [phrasal verbs](phrasal-verbs.md))
    **You're done when:** you can produce the correct preposition for 40 work verbs/nouns/adjectives from memory and you have a personal "preposition trap" list of your 15 most frequent errors.

## Why it matters
Prepositions in English are largely lexical: they are attached to specific words, and there is no reliable logical rule ("discuss about" is wrong, "talk about" is right, and both mean the same). Fluent speakers transfer prepositions from their first language ("depend from", "explain me", "different than/of", "emphasize on"). Each error is small; together they make a Staff-level document feel unpolished. Because it is memorisation, the efficient method is **chunk learning**: store the verb with its preposition and a work example, and review by spaced repetition.

## Core concepts

### Three layers of preposition knowledge
1. **Time and place prepositions** (rule-based): *at / on / in*.
2. **Dependent prepositions** (lexical): verb/noun/adjective + fixed preposition (*rely on, a solution to, responsible for*).
3. **Prepositional phrases / fixed expressions** (*in production, on call, at risk, by design, under review*).

### Time: at / on / in / by / for / since / during / within

| Preposition | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **at** | Clock time, points, night | at 09:30, at midnight, at the end of the sprint, at that point |
| **on** | Days and dates, specific occasions | on Monday, on 3 March (US: on March 3), on the day of the release |
| **in** | Months, years, quarters, longer periods; "after a period" | in Q3, in 2027, in the morning, "ready in two days" (= after two days) |
| **by** | Deadline: no later than | "Submit by Friday", "by end of day (EOD)" |
| **until / till** | Continuing up to a time | "We freeze until Friday" (freeze continues) vs "We freeze by Friday" (freeze starts no later than) |
| **for** | Duration | "for three weeks", "for a long time" |
| **since** | Start point, continuing to now (perfect tenses) | "since 2022", "since the last release" |
| **during** | Throughout/at some point in an event | "during the incident", "during the review" |
| **within** | Not longer than | "within 24 hours", "within budget", "within scope" |
| **from ... to / through** | Range; US *through* is inclusive | "Monday through Friday" (US) vs "Monday to Friday" (UK) |
| **over / across** | A span with change | "over the past year", "across three sprints" |
| **on time / in time** | *on time* = punctual; *in time* = early enough | "The train was on time" / "We shipped in time for the audit" |
| **at the end / in the end** | *at the end (of)* = last point; *in the end* = eventually | "At the end of the call..." / "In the end, we rolled back." |

**Fluent-speaker traps:** "in Monday" (no: *on Monday*); "since three weeks" (no: *for three weeks*); "during three days" (no: *for three days*); "until" vs "by" confusion in deadlines; "within" vs "in" ("in 24 hours" = after 24 hours, "within 24 hours" = at most 24).

### Place and abstract place: at / in / on / to

| Pattern | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| in + enclosed or large area | in the office, in Rotterdam, in the cloud, in the repo, in the pipeline | |
| on + surface, platform, channel, list | on the roadmap, on Slack, on the call, on the agenda, on GitHub, on AWS | *on* for platforms and services. |
| at + point/event | at the office (as a location), at a conference, at the meeting, at scale | "at" points; "in the meeting" = in the room. Both accepted. |
| to + destination | "Send it to Anna", "deploy to production", "migrate to Postgres" | Never "arrive to" (*arrive in* a city, *at* a place). |
| Motion verbs | "reach the goal" (no preposition), "enter the room" (no prep), "attend the meeting" (no prep), "join the call" | |

| Verb + preposition | Example | Common error |

### -ing after prepositions
After a preposition the verb takes **-ing** (gerund): "focus on **reducing** costs", "committed to **shipping**", "the risk of **missing** the date", "instead of **rewriting**", "look forward to **hearing** from you" (*to* is a preposition here). Exception: no infinitive after a preposition (except *but/except* and a few fixed cases).

### Collocation as a system
A **collocation** is a pair or group of words that native speakers combine by convention. Learn them in chunks by type:

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Verb + noun | *make a decision, raise a concern, reach consensus, meet a deadline, run a test, address an issue* |
| Adjective + noun | *heavy load, tight deadline, strong case, viable option, steep learning curve* |
| Adverb + adjective | *highly available, fully managed, deeply nested, widely adopted* |
| Noun + noun | *a decision framework, a risk register, a deployment window* |
| Verb + preposition | *rely on, comply with, adhere to* |
| Preposition + noun (fixed) | *in production, at risk, on hold, under review, by design, out of scope* |

Method: when you learn a word, also note (a) what verb pairs with it, (b) its adjective, (c) its preposition. Use ozdic.com or an Oxford/Cambridge dictionary entry to check. See [collocations & precision](collocations-precision.md) for numbers and data.

### "Different from / than / to", "compared to/with", "based on"
- **different from** is the safest everywhere. *Different to* is UK informal; *different than* is US and natural before a clause ("different than it was last year").
- **compare with** = examine similarities and differences; **compare to** = liken ("compared to a monolith, microservices...") but both are used interchangeably in practice; *compared with/to* at the start of a sentence is fine.
- **based on** (not "on the basis of" unless you want formal) and **due to** (noun-cause: "The delay was due to a bug") vs **because of** (adverbial: "We were late because of a bug").

## Hands-on lab (45-60 min)
1. **Trap list.** Look at your last 20 sent emails or Slack posts. Highlight each verb/noun/adjective + preposition. Check each against the Oxford Learner's dictionary. List every mismatch. That is your personal trap list.
2. **Flashcard set.** Create 30 cards in the format `rely ___ retries → on` with a work sentence. Include 10 from your trap list. Review with spaced repetition over 14 days (Anki or a simple Leitner box).
3. **Ozdic drill.** For five nouns you use daily (*decision, risk, deadline, incident, trade-off*), look up verb collocates and write one real sentence per pairing.
4. **Corpus check.** In COCA, compare "different from / than / to" frequency and "impact on / to".
5. **Write a paragraph** (150 words) about your current project using at least 10 dependent prepositions; have someone or a tool check it.

## Questions

### L1 - Recall

??? question "Q1. Give the correct preposition: (a) depend ___ the SLA; (b) responsible ___ the migration; (c) a solution ___ the problem; (d) an increase ___ latency; (e) an increase ___ 20%."
    ??? success "Answer"
        (a) on; (b) for; (c) to; (d) in; (e) of. *In* introduces the thing that changes; *of* the amount.

??? question "Q2. Which of these are correct? \"discuss about the plan\", \"emphasize on quality\", \"explain me the design\", \"we agreed on the date\", \"attend to the meeting\""
    ??? success "Answer"
        Only "we agreed on the date" is correct. Correct forms: *discuss the plan*, *emphasize quality* (or *put emphasis on quality*), *explain the design to me*, *attend the meeting* (*attend to* means "deal with").

??? question "Q3. What is the difference between \"by Friday\" and \"until Friday\"?"
    ??? success "Answer"
        *By Friday* = no later than Friday (a deadline). *Until Friday* = continuing up to Friday (duration). "Submit by Friday", "The freeze lasts until Friday."

??? question "Q4. After \"look forward to\", \"committed to\" and \"focus on\", do you use the infinitive or -ing? Why?"
    ??? success "Answer"
        -ing. In each phrase *to/on* is a preposition, and prepositions take a noun or gerund: "look forward to hearing", "committed to shipping", "focus on reducing".

### L2 - Apply

??? question "Q5. Correct the errors: \"We discussed about the outage during two hours and the CTO asked to us about the root cause. We depend from a third-party vendor and we are aware about the risk.\""
    ??? success "Answer"
        "We discussed the outage **for** two hours and the CTO asked **us** about the root cause. We depend **on** a third-party vendor and we are aware **of** the risk." (*Discuss* takes no preposition; *during* + a measure of time is wrong, use *for*; *ask someone about*; *depend on*; *aware of*.)

??? question "Q6. Fill: \"I'm not familiar ___ this framework, but the concept is familiar ___ me from a previous project. Our team is capable ___ handling the load, but prone ___ alert fatigue, so we're committed ___ reducing noise.\""
    ??? success "Answer"
        familiar **with**; familiar **to** me; capable **of** handling; prone **to** alert fatigue; committed **to** reducing. *Familiar with* = you know it; *familiar to* = it is known to you.

??? question "Q7. Choose in / on / at: (a) ___ Q4; (b) ___ the roadmap; (c) ___ scale; (d) ___ the call (attending); (e) ___ the airport; (f) ___ 9:30."
    ??? success "Answer"
        (a) in Q4; (b) on the roadmap; (c) at scale; (d) on the call; (e) at the airport; (f) at 9:30.

??? question "Q8. Rewrite with the right preposition or structure: \"The delay was resulted from a wrong estimation, and it will result to customers complain.\""
    ??? success "Answer"
        "The delay **resulted from** an incorrect estimate, and it will **result in** customer complaints." *Result from* = cause; *result in* = effect; the original also had wrong verb forms and *complain* (verb) instead of *complaints* (noun).

### L3 - Judge and choose

??? question "Q9. \"Different from\", \"different to\" and \"different than\": which do you pick in a globally distributed team, and when is *than* clearly better?"
    ??? success "Answer"
        *Different from* is safe in both varieties and formal writing. *Different than* is natural in American English before a clause ("Prod behaves differently than it did in staging" / "It's different than what we expected"), where "different from what" is longer. *Different to* is British; avoid it in a document with US readers, though it is not an error.

??? question "Q10. \"The impact to customers\" vs \"the impact on customers\": which do you write in a postmortem and why?"
    ??? success "Answer"
        *Impact on customers* is the traditional and safe form; many editors treat *impact to* as jargon. In US tech and incident vocabulary *impact to* is widespread and understood, so it is not "wrong", but if you write for a mixed or formal audience, choose *on*. Use *affect* or *effect on* if you want to avoid the debate. Consistency within a document matters more than the choice.

??? question "Q11. \"Reply the email\", \"reply to the email\" and \"respond the email\": which are correct?"
    ??? success "Answer"
        Only *reply to the email* and *respond to the email* (both need *to*). "Reply" can also be intransitive ("I'll reply tomorrow") or take a quotation ("'Yes,' he replied").

??? question "Q12. \"We're moving from Oracle to Postgres\" / \"We're migrating off Oracle onto Postgres\": what nuance does each carry?"
    ??? success "Answer"
        *Move from X to Y* is neutral. *Migrate off X onto Y* is idiomatic in engineering speech and stresses leaving X (motivation to escape) and landing on Y (platform). "Off" is informal-professional. Neither is an error; prefer *from...to* in formal documents.

### L4 - Real-world decisions

??? question "Q13. You are reviewing a junior colleague's RFC full of preposition errors. How do you give feedback that is useful and not demoralising, and what do you actually fix?"
    ??? success "Answer"
        Fix errors that change meaning or look careless in a document that many people will read (*depend from*, *discuss about*, the *by/until* deadline confusion, *result in/from* direction). Ignore or leave alone the ones that are only stylistic (*different to/than*). Give a short pattern list ("here are the 5 patterns I corrected; there are also...") rather than a full markup, and link a resource. Pair that with a comment on the content first, so the language note is not the headline. Non-native reviewers' comments should also be normalised: you can share your own trap list to reduce the stigma.

??? question "Q14. A stakeholder email asks: \"Can you confirm the deployment will be done until Monday?\" You know the intended meaning is a deadline. How do you reply without correcting them in an awkward way?"
    ??? success "Answer"
        Echo the correct form naturally: "Yes, the deployment will be done **by** Monday." Mirroring is unobtrusive and models the correct form. Only if a misunderstanding is possible (until Monday = through Monday, could mean it runs to Monday) do you ask explicitly: "Just to confirm: complete no later than Monday morning, correct?"

??? question "Q15. You need to state a deadline for a global team without ambiguity. Compare \"within 24 hours\", \"in 24 hours\", \"by EOD Friday\", \"by 17:00 CET Friday\"."
    ??? success "Answer"
        *Within 24 hours* = at most 24 hours from now (clear). *In 24 hours* = after 24 hours (a future point), often read as the same, so avoid. *By EOD Friday* is ambiguous across time zones ("whose EOD?"). *By 17:00 CET Friday* (or with a UTC time) is unambiguous. Use the last one for cross-region commitments.

## Real-world use cases
- **RFC and design-doc review.** Prepositions after *depend/rely/agree/comment* appear in every proposal; consistent editing improves credibility.
- **Incident timelines.** "At 09:32 the alert fired; by 09:45 traffic had shifted to the DR region; until 10:20 latency remained elevated": *at/by/until* each carry a distinct meaning.
- **SLA language.** "Within 4 business hours", "subject to approval", "in accordance with clause 3": legal-flavoured fixed phrases.
- **Interview answers.** "I was responsible for X, accountable to Y, and contributed to Z" - precise verbs with correct prepositions signal seniority.
- **Slack.** *On the call, on the thread, in the channel, in the doc, on the doc* (both are heard; *in* for content, *on* for the document as a place): observe and mirror the local norm.

## Pitfalls & anti-patterns
- Translating prepositions from your first language.
- Learning prepositions in isolation rather than in chunks with a sentence.
- Assuming a preposition is "logical"; it isn't (*discuss X* vs *talk about X*).
- Overcorrecting: *impact to*, *different than* are debated, not wrong.
- Using *until* for deadlines and *for* for points.
- Forgetting that verbs of communication take an indirect object with *to* (*explain, suggest, mention, say, propose, describe X to Y*).
- Using *since* with a duration ("since three weeks").
