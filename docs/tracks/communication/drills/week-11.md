## Day 1 — Grammar: Advanced articles and the zero article with abstract nouns {#day-1}
**⏱ 30 min · Mon** Week 1 covered the basics of `a/the/zero`. This week is the hard part: abstract nouns and fixed phrases, where fluent speakers still slip.

### Rule in 60 seconds

**1. Generic abstract and uncountable nouns take no article.** `Reliability matters more than speed`, `Security is a shared responsibility`, `Latency is critical for users`, `Trust takes years to build`. Generic plural nouns also take zero: `Engineers dislike meetings.`

**2. Add `the` when the noun is specified or unique.** A specifying phrase (`of ...`, a relative clause, a limiting adjective) makes the noun definite:

- `**The** reliability **of our platform** is our priority.`
- `**The** trust **we built with the carriers** took years.`
- `**The** latency **in the checkout flow** doubled.`

**3. Use `a/an` with abstract nouns when they are qualified as one instance or kind:** `a deep understanding of the domain`, `a growing need for automation`, `a lack of trust`, `a high degree of uncertainty`, `a reasonable expectation`. Without the qualifier, they are zero: `We need understanding`, `There is need for automation` is unusual.

**4. Fixed phrases (learn as chunks).**

| Zero article | With `the` |
|---|---|
| in production, in development, in practice, in theory | in the cloud, in the meantime, in the end |
| at scale, at risk, at work, at speed | on the whole, on the other hand |
| on time, in time, in advance, in detail | at the moment, at the same time |
| by email, by phone, by hand, by design | the internet (also `the web`), the market, the board |
| under pressure, in charge, on call | in the loop (idiom), out of the office |

**5. Unique or shared reference takes `the`:** `the CTO`, `the board`, `the customer` (the one we discussed), `the data` (specific data set). Generic: `data` (`Data drives decisions`).

**6. Product and proper names:** zero for most (`Kubernetes`, `Kafka`, `Terraform`), but `the` before a generic noun after the name (`the Kubernetes API`, `the Jira ticket`). Countries: zero (`Germany`), except `the Netherlands`, `the UK`, `the US`.

**The errors fluent speakers make:**

- Over-inserting `the` for generic ideas: `The latency is critical for the users` (generic; should be `Latency is critical for users`).
- `in the production` (wrong: `in production`, unless `the production environment`).
- Adding `a` to uncountable nouns: `a documentation`, `an information`. Use `some documentation`, `a piece of information`.
- Dropping the article before a qualified abstract noun: `We have great need for automation` should be `a great need for` (though `great need` is also found in formal writing).
- `by the email`: means of communication needs zero (`by email`).

### Exercise

Correct each sentence or fill the gap (10). Write `-` for zero article.

2. We need a documentation for the API.

??? success "Answers and explanations"
    1. `Latency is critical for users.` Both nouns are generic.
    2. `We need **documentation** for the API` (uncountable). `A piece of documentation` is possible but rarely needed.
    3. **-** and **-**: `Reliability matters more than speed.` Generic abstract nouns.
    4. `The service is running **in production**.` Fixed phrase.
    5. **a**: `a deep understanding of...`, qualified abstract noun.
    6. **-**: `by email`, means of communication.
    7. **Trust**: generic idea.

### Use it

## Day 2 — Vocabulary: Register: formal vs informal, and switching {#day-2}
**⏱ 30 min · Tue** Register is the level of formality. In global enterprises you move between Slack, a design review and a board paper in one day.

| Word | Part of speech | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| ascertain | verb | To find out something with certainty; formal for `find out` or `check`. | We need to ascertain whether the data was encrypted at rest before we notify the regulator. |
| notwithstanding | preposition | Despite; formal, used in contracts and formal reports; can go before or after its noun. | Notwithstanding the vendor's delay, the go-live date remains 14 March. |
| expedite | verb | To make a process happen faster; formal for `speed up`. | Could you expedite the security review, as the launch date is fixed? |
| reiterate | verb | To say again, for emphasis or clarity. | I want to reiterate that no customer data was exposed. |
| forthright | adjective | Direct and honest in speech; more formal than `candid`, often used to describe someone who states an opinion plainly. | The VP was forthright about which two features would slip. |

### Collocations and word family

- **ascertain:** ascertain the facts / cause / extent / whether; noun `ascertainment` is rare; use `determine` in similar formal contexts. Not used with people (`ascertain John`).
- **notwithstanding:** notwithstanding the above / this / any objections / anything in section 4; also postposed: `objections notwithstanding`. Do not use in casual writing.
- **expedite:** expedite a request / delivery / approval / process; noun `expedition` (a journey) is a different word; adjective `expeditious` (formal).
- **reiterate:** reiterate a point / commitment / concern / that; noun `reiteration`; `repeat` is the neutral choice.
- **forthright:** a forthright answer / manner / assessment; adverb `forthrightly` (rare, prefer `in a forthright way`); do not confuse with `forthcoming` (willing to give information, or *about to happen*).

**Register ladder:** find out (informal) < check < establish < ascertain / determine (formal). Speed up < accelerate < expedite. Start < commence. Help < assist. Use `commence`, `ascertain`, `aforementioned` only when a document requires legal or highly formal tone; overuse makes writing stilted.

### Exercise

Fill each gap (8). Review words: **contentious, leverage, negligible**.

1. Please ______ the cause of the failure before we brief the customer.
2. ______ the delay, we still expect to meet the deadline.
3. Could you ______ the approval, since the vendor's quote expires on Friday?
4. I would like to ______ that the deadline has not changed.
5. Give me your ______ opinion: is the team ready? (more formal than `candid`)
6. Which register is `Let's find out what went wrong`: informal or formal? Rewrite formally.
7. Ownership of the shared queue is a ______ topic between the two teams.
8. The impact on latency is ______, so we can proceed.

??? success "Answers and explanations"
    1. **ascertain** (find out with certainty), or `determine`.
    2. **Notwithstanding**: `despite`, formal.
    3. **expedite**: speed up.
    4. **reiterate**: say again.
    5. **forthright**: direct and honest, a formal register choice.
    6. **Informal.** Formal: `We need to ascertain what went wrong.` / `We must establish the cause.`
    7. **contentious** (review, Week 8).
    8. **negligible** (review, Week 9). `Leverage` (review, Week 10) is not a fit here: it needs an object.

### Use it
Pick one Slack-style sentence you wrote today and rewrite it in a formal register (for a board paper). Then rewrite a stiff email sentence in a friendly register. Say both aloud. The goal is speed in switching, not loving the formal version.

## Day 3 — Speaking: Clarity on calls and video: pacing, signposting {#day-3}
**⏱ 30 min · Wed** Audio compresses on calls: delays, echoes and accents make speech harder to parse, so slow down and structure more.

**Prompt (60-90 seconds):** In a distributed team meeting, give a status update on one project. Start with the headline, use spoken signposts, check that people can hear you, and end with what you need.

**Structure: Headline, Signpost, Body, Check, Ask**

1. **Headline (10 s):** `The headline is: we are on track for 14 March, with one risk.`
2. **Signpost:** `I'll cover three things: progress, the risk, and what I need from you.`
3. **Body:** short sentences; number each point aloud (`First..., Second..., Third...`).
4. **Check:** `Can everyone hear me OK?` / `Any questions so far?` / `Let me pause there.`
5. **Ask:** `What I need from this group is a decision on the vendor by Friday.`

**Pacing on calls:** aim for about 130-150 words per minute (a normal conversational pace is faster), pause 1-2 seconds after key numbers and after each signpost, and repeat critical figures (`fourteen, one-four, March`).

**Pronunciation micro-drill: `can` vs `can't` and linking**

- `can` is weak in statements: /kən/ (`We can meet the date`). `can't` is strong: US `/kænt/` with clear /æ/ and a stop, UK `/kɑːnt/`. On a bad line, weak `can` and strong `can't` are easily confused. Fix: stress the meaningful word and lengthen `can't`: `We CAN'T meet the date`. Better, when it matters: `We are not able to...` or `We will not meet the date.`
- **Linking (consonant to vowel):** say `check it out` as `check_it_out`, `turn it off` as `turn_it_off`, `an apple` as `a-napple`, `as soon as possible` as `a-ssoon-a-spossible`. Practice with: `pick up all issues`, `look at it again`, `roll it out on Monday`, `a lot of it`.
- **Chunking:** group words in thought groups of 3-6 words: `The headline is / we are on track / for the fourteenth of March.` Pause between groups.

**Self-check**

- [ ] I stated the headline first.
- [ ] I used at least three signposts (`First`, `Second`, `Finally`, `Let me turn to`).
- [ ] I paused after key numbers and after signposts.
- [ ] My `can't` was clearly different from `can`.
- [ ] I checked audience understanding at least once.

## Day 4 — Idioms & phrasal verbs: Escalation phrasal verbs: flag up, push back, loop in {#day-4}
**⏱ 30 min · Thu**

| Expression | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| flag up | Bring attention to a problem or issue; `flag up` is more UK, and `flag` alone (`flag a risk`) is common in US and UK. Neutral. | Please flag up any blockers in the stand-up so we can help. |
| push back (noun: pushback) | Resist or object to a request or plan; can be constructive. Neutral in business. | The security team pushed back on the timeline, and they were right. |
| loop in | Include someone in a conversation or email chain; informal to neutral; opposite `leave out of the loop`. | Can you loop in legal before we send the contract? |
| bubble up | Rise to the attention of senior people, or move upward through an organization; informal. | Concerns about the migration were starting to bubble up to the leadership team. |
| weigh in | Contribute an opinion to a discussion, often a decision; informal to neutral. | The CTO weighed in on the architecture choice after the review. |

**Grammar:** `loop in` is separable (`loop legal in` / `loop in legal`), `push back` is intransitive with `on` (`push back on the estimate`), `flag up` is separable (`flag it up`), `weigh in` is intransitive with `on` (`weigh in on the decision`), `bubble up` is intransitive (`it bubbled up`). Pronouns go in the middle: `loop them in`.

### Exercise

Choose or complete (8).

1. Rewrite: `Please tell the team about any blockers.` (using `flag up`)
2. `Can you ______ the compliance team on this email?` (loop in / push back)
3. Correct: `We need to loop in her before the meeting.`
4. A colleague says `I will push back on that estimate.` What are they going to do?
5. Rewrite plainly: `Concerns are bubbling up to the executive team.`
6. Complete: `Product wanted to ______ on the architecture decision, though they are not part of the review.` (weigh in / loop in)
7. Which is more assertive: `push back` or `flag`?
8. Fix: `I will push it back on them.`

??? success "Answers and explanations"
    1. `Please **flag up** any blockers.` (US: `flag any blockers`.)
    2. **loop in**.
    3. `We need to **loop her in** before the meeting.` A pronoun object must go between verb and particle (a noun can go either side: `loop in legal`).
    4. They will object to or challenge the estimate; often constructive, so they may propose a better number.
    5. `Senior leaders are beginning to hear about the concerns.`
    6. **weigh in**: give an opinion.
    7. **push back**: it resists or challenges; `flag` simply draws attention.
    8. `I will **push back on** them` (or `push back on that`). `Push back` takes `on` plus the thing pushed against; it does not take `it`.

!!! warning "When NOT to use them"
    `Push back` can sound confrontational in cultures where direct disagreement is uncommon; `raise a concern` is safer in first-time contact. `Loop in` and `bubble up` are corporate-internal jargon and can confuse non-native readers; in formal escalation to executives or customers, write `include` and `escalate`. Do not use `flag up` in a US audience unless you are comfortable with a UK feel; `flag` is fine.

## Day 5 — Writing: Escalation emails {#day-5}
**⏱ 30 min · Fri**

**Bad draft:**

> Hi Tom, this is the third time I am asking about the API access for our vendor integration and honestly it is unacceptable. Your team keeps ignoring my tickets and this is putting our whole project at risk. I have cc'd your boss because someone needs to do something. Please fix this immediately.

**Task:** Rewrite as a firm but professional escalation email to a peer team's manager, 110-150 words. Constraints: neutral subject line; state facts and dates; state business impact with a number; make one clear request with a deadline; no blame words; register formal-neutral (use one of `expedite`, `ascertain`, `reiterate`).

??? success "Model answer and what changed"
    > **Subject: Request to expedite API access for vendor integration (needed by 12 Oct)**
    >
    > Hi Tom,
    >
    > I am writing to follow up on ticket INT-4412, submitted on 22 September, and two subsequent reminders on 29 September and 3 October. We have not yet received a response. I understand your team is handling a heavy queue.
    >
    > The access is needed to keep the vendor integration on schedule. Without it by 12 October, the go-live date of 1 November is at risk, which affects roughly 40 customers. Could your team expedite the request, or let me know by Wednesday if there is a blocker we can help ascertain?
    >
    > I have copied Sara, as the request is now close to a critical-path date. Please tell me if you would like to talk it through.
    >
    > Thanks,
    > Rahul

    - **Subject line contains the ask and the date** so the manager can triage without opening.
    - **Facts and dates replace emotion:** ticket number, three dates, no `unacceptable`.
    - **Impact is quantified** (`roughly 40 customers`, go-live date).
    - **Acknowledges the other side** (`heavy queue`), which keeps the door open.
    - **One request and one deadline,** with an alternative if there is a blocker.
    - **The cc is explained**, not used as a threat.
    - **Register:** formal-neutral, with `expedite` and `ascertain` used naturally; no idioms.

## Day 7 — Soft skills + weekly review: Saying no without burning bridges {#day-7}
**⏱ 30 min · Sun**

**Scenario.** Your head of sales, Jorge, has a strategic customer who wants a custom reporting feature in two weeks. Building it would derail the platform migration you promised finance for this quarter. Jorge says: "This customer is worth 8 million a year. I need a yes." You cannot say yes, and you value the relationship.

**Task:** Script your 150-word reply, keeping the relationship intact.

??? success "Model response and techniques"
    > Jorge, I understand what is at stake, and I want to help win this customer. The honest answer is that I cannot deliver a custom report in two weeks without putting the migration at risk, and the migration is what protects the revenue of every existing customer, including this one. So the answer to the two-week version is no. Here is what I can do. I can give you a manual, weekly export in five days that covers about 80 percent of what they want, and I can scope the full feature for the start of next quarter with a committed date. Could you check with the customer whether the export would meet their immediate need? If they need the full feature sooner, let us raise it together with finance, who set the migration priority, so the trade-off is a shared decision.

    **Techniques used**

    - **Acknowledge the stake and the goal** before saying no.
    - **Clear, early no to the specific request** (`the answer to the two-week version is no`), not to the person, and without hedging.
    - **Reason tied to a shared interest** (protecting revenue).
    - **Alternatives with dates and scope** (export in five days, full feature next quarter).
    - **Ask for information** (what does the customer need) to invite collaboration.
    - **Escalate the trade-off, not the conflict** (`raise it together with finance`).
