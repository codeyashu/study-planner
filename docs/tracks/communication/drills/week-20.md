## Day 1 — Grammar: mixed advanced structures {#day-1}
**⏱ 30 min · Mon**

### Rule in 60 seconds

Seven advanced structures that mark C1-C2 writing and speech. Learn the pattern, then the trap.

| Structure | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Cleft (it-cleft) | `It was + focus + that/who ...` | "It was the stale cache that caused the outage." |
| Wh-cleft | `What + clause + is/was + focus` | "What we need is a clear owner." |
| Negative inversion | Negative adverbial + auxiliary + subject | "Rarely do we see such clean data." "Only after the postmortem did we understand the cause." "Hardly had the deploy finished when the alerts fired." |
| Subjunctive after recommend/insist/suggest/essential | `that + subject + base verb` (no -s, no will) | "I recommend that we postpone the release." "It is essential that she approve it." |
| Mixed conditional | `If + past perfect, would + base verb` (past cause, present result) | "If we had added an index, the queries wouldn't be slow now." |
| Inverted conditional (formal) | `Should / Were / Had + subject ...` | "Should the vendor miss the deadline, we will invoke the penalty clause." "Had we known, we would have escalated." |
| Prepositional relative | `preposition + whom / whose + noun` | "The team on whose approval we depend is in Singapore." |

**Traps:**
- `Hardly/scarcely ... when`, `no sooner ... than`: not "hardly ... than".
- After a negative or `only + adverbial`, the auxiliary comes *before* the subject (`did we understand`); forgetting it is the most common error.
- `Recommend that we will/should`: British English accepts `should` ("I recommend that we should postpone"); American English and formal writing prefer the bare subjunctive. Never `will` and never `-s` ("that she approves" is common in speech but non-standard in formal registers).
- Mixed conditionals: mix only when the times differ; do not use them when both are past.

**The error fluent speakers make:** applying inversion in the wrong place ("Only we realized after..."), or using cleft sentences everywhere: one per paragraph is emphasis, three is a tic.

### Exercise

1. Rewrite as an it-cleft emphasizing the cause: "The stale cache caused the outage."
2. Correct: "Only after the postmortem we understood the cause."
3. Correct: "Hardly had the deploy finished than the alerts fired."
4. Correct: "I recommend that we will postpone the release."
5. Combine into one mixed conditional: "We did not add an index. The queries are slow now."
6. Correct: "The team whose approval we depend is in Singapore."
7. Rewrite as a wh-cleft: "We need a clear owner."
8. Choose: "Rarely (we see / do we see) such clean data."
9. Combine with a participle clause and passive: "The API was designed for internal use. It was later exposed to partners. It now lacks rate limits."
10. Rewrite formally with inversion: "If the vendor should miss the deadline, we will invoke the penalty clause."

??? success "Answers and explanations"
    1. **"It was the stale cache that caused the outage."**
    2. **"Only after the postmortem did we understand the cause."** Inversion after `only + adverbial`.
    3. **"...when the alerts fired."** The pattern is `hardly ... when` (or `no sooner ... than`).
    4. **"I recommend that we postpone the release."** Subjunctive: base form after recommend; `should postpone` is acceptable in British English.
    5. **"If we had added an index, the queries wouldn't be slow now."** Past condition, present result.
    6. **"The team on whose approval we depend..."** (or "whose approval we depend on"). The preposition must accompany the relative pronoun in the formal version.
    7. **"What we need is a clear owner."**
    8. **do we see.** Negative adverb `rarely` triggers inversion.
    9. **"Designed for internal use and later exposed to partners, the API now lacks rate limits."**
    10. **"Should the vendor miss the deadline, we will invoke the penalty clause."**

### Use it
Write three sentences about your project: one it-cleft, one with `Only after ... did`, one inverted conditional (`Should ...`). Read them aloud; check each has correct word order.

## Day 2 — Vocabulary: review and 100-word check {#day-2}
**⏱ 30 min · Tue**

### Part A: five new words

| Word | Part of speech | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| multifaceted | adjective | Having many different sides or aspects; complex, not reducible to one factor | "The outage was multifaceted: a config error, a missed alert and an on-call gap all played a part." |
| pivotal | adjective | Of central importance; a turning point on which outcomes depend | "The pilot was pivotal in winning executive support." |
| enshrine | verb | To formally set down and protect a rule, right or principle, often in a document or policy | "We enshrined the on-call handover in the team's runbook." |
| harmonize | verb | To make different things consistent or compatible with each other | "Finance could not harmonize the invoice with the usage report." |
| underpin | verb | To support or form the basis of something | "A shared event schema underpins every integration on the platform." |

Collocations: a multifaceted problem / issue / challenge; a pivotal role / moment / decision / figure; enshrine a right / principle / rule (in law / in policy); harmonize A with B / harmonize standards / harmonize data; underpin a strategy / an argument / the economy / a system. Word family: facet / multifaceted; pivot / pivotal; enshrine / enshrined; harmonize / harmonization; underpinning (noun). Pronunciation: mul-ti-FASS-et-ed, PIV-uh-tl, en-SHRINE, HAR-mo-nize, un-der-PIN.

## Day 3 — Speaking: a 10-minute technical talk {#day-3}
**⏱ 30 min · Wed**

**Prompt.** Plan a 10-minute talk on a technical topic you know well (for example, "Designing idempotent event consumers"). Today: build the outline in 10 minutes and deliver the *first two minutes* (hook and roadmap) aloud in 20 minutes. Saturday you will record a compressed version.

**Structure and time budget**

| Segment | Time | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | 0:45 | A failure, a number or a question |
| Roadmap | 0:45 | "I'll cover three things: what broke, what we built, what we'd change" |
| Section 1 | 2:30 | Problem and constraints |
| Section 2 | 3:00 | Solution and one trade-off (with a diagram) |
| Section 3 | 1:30 | Results and lessons |
| Summary + call to action | 1:00 | Restate three points; one thing to try |
| Buffer | 0:30 | Pauses and transitions |

Use signposts: "That brings me to...", "The second thing is...", "To summarize...". Use an it-cleft or a wh-cleft once for emphasis ("What surprised us was...").

## Day 4 — Idioms & phrasal verbs: idiom review round {#day-4}
**⏱ 30 min · Thu**

| Expression | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| read the room | To sense the mood of a group and adjust your behavior accordingly. Informal but common in business. | "I was about to ask for budget, then I read the room and waited." |
| go back to the drawing board | To start again after a plan has failed. Informal; neutral in tone, slightly self-deprecating. | "The load test failed, so it's back to the drawing board." |
| play devil's advocate | To argue an opposing view deliberately, to test an idea. Neutral; say it aloud so people know your intent. | "Let me play devil's advocate: what if the vendor never delivers?" |
| cut to the chase | To get to the main point without preamble. Informal; can sound abrupt. | "To cut to the chase: we need two more engineers or a later date." |
| the price of admission | The minimum thing you must accept or provide to be allowed to compete or participate. Neutral to informal, US and UK. | "Multi-region failover is the price of admission for this customer." |

### Exercise (8 items)

1. Fill in: "The executives look tense; let's ______ before we mention the cost."
2. Fill in: "The prototype failed; we're going ______."
3. Fill in: "I'll ______: does anyone believe this schedule is realistic?"
4. Fill in: "Encryption at rest is ______ for enterprise customers."
5. Choose the best: "We're short on time, so let me ______." (cut to the chase / read the room)
6. Review (week 16): "The decision is unavoidable, so we might as well ______." (bite the bullet / cut corners)
7. Review (week 19): "The pilot data ______ the skeptics." (won over / went back to the drawing board)
8. Rewrite more idiomatically: "Let me argue the opposite position to test the plan."

??? success "Answers and explanations"
    1. **read the room.** 2. **back to the drawing board.** 3. **play devil's advocate.** 4. **the price of admission.** 5. **cut to the chase**: get to the point. 6. **bite the bullet** (week 16). 7. **won over** (week 19). 8. "Let me **play devil's advocate**."

**When NOT to use them:** *cut to the chase* can sound dismissive if said to someone giving context; use it about *your* own preamble ("let me cut to the chase"), not to ask others to hurry. *The price of admission* can sound flippant about a serious requirement; explain it plainly for international audiences unfamiliar with the phrase. *Play devil's advocate* should be announced, otherwise colleagues may think you are opposing them.

## Day 5 — Writing: blog post final edit {#day-5}
**⏱ 30 min · Fri**

**Draft to edit** (a section from a blog post):

> It is important to note that the implementation of the new caching layer was carried out by our team over the course of a period of three weeks. The decision was made by the team to make use of Redis, due to the fact that it has the ability to provide very fast reads. There were several issues that were encountered during the migration, and the team was of the opinion that a rollback would be necessary. It should be noted that the rollback was performed successfully and the results that were obtained were very good.

**Task.** Do a final edit: cut at least 40 percent of the words, replace passive and nominalized phrases with strong active verbs, remove filler ("it is important to note"), and keep one participle clause and one contrast for rhythm. Keep the meaning. Then list the edits you made in five bullets.

??? success "Model answer"
    We built the new caching layer in three weeks. We chose Redis because it reads faster than the alternatives. Hitting several issues during the migration, we rolled back once, then relaunched successfully. Reads now average 3 ms, not 40.

    (Word count: about 45, from about 100, a 55 percent cut. If you kept the numbers vague, add real ones; the model invents "3 ms, not 40" as an example.)

    What changed and why:
    - **Filler removed:** "It is important to note that" and "It should be noted that" carry no information.
    - **Nominalizations became verbs:** "the implementation was carried out" became "we built"; "made use of" became "chose"; "has the ability to provide" became "reads faster".
    - **Passive became active** with "we", which shortens and clarifies responsibility.
    - **"Due to the fact that"** became "because"; "over the course of a period of three weeks" became "in three weeks".
    - **One participle clause** ("Hitting several issues...") and **one contrast** ("3 ms, not 40") give rhythm and a concrete payoff instead of "very good".

## Day 7 — Soft skills + weekly review: Q&A under pressure {#day-7}
**⏱ 30 min · Sun**

**Scenario.** After your technical talk, two questions arrive from the front row. (1) A director asks sharply: "Isn't this just reinventing Kafka? Why should we trust a homegrown solution?" (2) An engineer asks about the memory footprint at 100,000 partitions, which you have not measured.

**Task.** Script your answer to both questions in about 150 words in total.

??? success "Model response"
    "*[Pause, then paraphrase.]* So the question is whether we're duplicating Kafka and taking on unnecessary risk. It's a fair concern. There is overlap, and we did evaluate Kafka. What ruled it out was the operational cost for our team size, and our requirement for per-tenant ordering, which needed custom partitioning. We mitigated the homegrown risk by limiting the scope to one use case and open-sourcing the tests. Does that answer the trust question?

    *[Second question.]* Good question, and I don't have that number. We've tested up to 10,000 partitions, where memory grew linearly, roughly 2 MB per thousand. Extrapolating suggests around 200 MB at 100,000, but I'd want to verify it before you rely on that. I'll run the test this week and share the result; if you leave your email with me, I'll send it."

    **Techniques used:** (1) A pause and paraphrase gives thinking time and checks understanding. (2) The premise is corrected politely ("there is overlap, and we did evaluate") without saying "you're wrong". (3) Reasons before reassurance. (4) A check question closes the loop. (5) For the unknown: honest "I don't have that number", what you *do* know, a labeled extrapolation, and a dated follow-up, never a guess presented as fact. (6) Facts and numbers, no defensiveness.
