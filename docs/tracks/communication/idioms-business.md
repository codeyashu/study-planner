# Idioms for work: meetings, strategy, negotiation

!!! abstract "At a glance"
    **Priority:** P0 · **Complexity:** 3/5 · **Est. time:** 3 h · **Phase:** 1 · **Prereqs:** [business & tech-leadership vocabulary](business-tech-vocabulary.md)
    **You're done when:** you can use 30 of the 65 idioms below correctly in a real sentence, you know which are US/UK/informal, and you can say when NOT to use an idiom (e.g., with non-native audiences, in writing that will be translated).

## Why it matters
Idioms are how fluent workplace English signals ease and belonging: "let's touch base", "that's a bit of a stretch", "we're on the same page". They are genuinely current (checked against 2020s workplace usage, not textbook lists) and grouped by **situation**, because that's how you retrieve them in a meeting. The other half of the skill is judgment: idioms can confuse non-native listeners, sound too casual in writing, or not translate for a global audience. This page pairs each idiom with a register note and, where relevant, a plain alternative to use instead.

## Core concepts

### How to learn and vet an idiom
1. Confirm it is **current** (still used by native speakers under ~50 in 2020s workplace contexts) - avoid dated idioms from old textbooks (*it's raining cats and dogs*, *let the cat out of the bag* is still current, but *at the drop of a hat* is dated-ish; check with YouGlish/COCA).
2. Note **register**: informal, neutral-professional, or formal-avoid.
3. Note **US/UK** if it differs.
4. Know the **plain equivalent** so you can switch for clarity.
5. Use it **once you've heard it** in real use (podcast, meeting), not from a list alone.

### 1. Meetings and collaboration

| Expression | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| touch base | make brief contact to check status | "Let's touch base after the demo." (neutral-informal, very common) |
| on the same page | in agreement/aligned understanding | "Before we start, let's make sure we're on the same page." |
| circle back | return to a topic later | "Can we circle back to budget after the roadmap discussion?" (neutral; some find it a cliché) |
| take it offline | discuss privately after/outside the meeting | "Let's take that offline; it's a two-person issue." |
| put a pin in it | pause the topic, revisit later | "Good point - let's put a pin in it and come back after the break." (informal) |
| bring (something) to the table | contribute something valuable | "She brings deep infra experience to the table." |
| get the ball rolling | start something moving | "Let's get the ball rolling on the RFC." (informal) |
| loop someone in | include someone in communication | "Loop in Security before you finalise the design." (also a phrasal verb; see [phrasal verbs](phrasal-verbs.md)) |
| keep someone in the loop | keep informed | "Keep me in the loop on the vendor call." |
| pick someone's brain | ask for informal expert advice | "Can I pick your brain about Kafka partitioning?" (informal) |
| think outside the box | think creatively, beyond convention | Slightly clichéd but still current; safe in most contexts. |
| a rain check | postpone an invitation | "Can I take a rain check on lunch?" (informal, US origin, now global) |

### 2. Progress, delay and status

| Expression | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| behind schedule | later than planned | "We're two weeks behind schedule." (neutral, safe, prefer this over idioms in status reports) |
| move the needle | make a measurable difference | "This change won't move the needle on latency." |
| in the pipeline | planned or in progress | "Three features are in the pipeline for Q2." |
| up in the air | undecided | "The budget for next quarter is still up in the air." |
| on track / off track | proceeding as planned / not | "The migration is on track for June." |
| a moving target | goals that keep changing | "Scope has been a moving target this sprint." |
| back to square one | starting over after failure | "The vendor cancelled, so we're back to square one." |
| hit the ground running | start productively right away | "She hit the ground running in her first sprint." |
| dead in the water | completely stalled, no hope of progress | "Without funding, the project is dead in the water." (fairly strong; use with care) |
| light at the end of the tunnel | a sign that a hard situation is nearly over | "After three outages, there's finally light at the end of the tunnel." |
| the tip of the iceberg | a small visible part of a much bigger problem | "This bug is the tip of the iceberg; the whole module needs a rewrite." |
| a moving part | one component in a larger, complex system | "There are a lot of moving parts in this migration." |

### 3. Strategy and vision

| Expression | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| low-hanging fruit | easy wins, quick to achieve | "Let's grab the low-hanging fruit before the harder refactors." (very common; slightly overused) |
| the big picture | the overall strategic view | "Don't lose sight of the big picture." |
| a north star | a guiding long-term goal | "Customer trust is our north star metric." (originally from product/growth; now common) |
| move the goalposts | change requirements after work has started (usually negative) | "Engineering is frustrated because leadership keeps moving the goalposts." |
| a game changer | something that significantly alters the situation | "This partnership is a game changer for us." (a bit hype; use sparingly) |
| boil the ocean | attempt something far too large/ambitious | "Let's not boil the ocean; ship the MVP first." |
| a quick win | a fast, low-effort improvement | "We need a quick win to rebuild trust with the client." |
| raise the bar | set a higher standard | "This release raises the bar for reliability." |
| a stretch goal | an ambitious, optional target beyond the main goal | "100% coverage is a stretch goal, not a requirement." |
| move (the company) forward | make progress | |
| a step in the right direction | partial progress towards a goal | "It's not perfect, but it's a step in the right direction." |

### 4. Risk and problems

| Expression | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| a red flag | a warning sign | "The vendor's slow response is a red flag." |
| a ticking time bomb | a problem that will explode later if unaddressed | "That unpatched dependency is a ticking time bomb." |
| the elephant in the room | an obvious problem nobody is addressing | "The elephant in the room is that nobody owns this service." |
| a blessing in disguise | something that seemed bad but turned out well | "Losing that vendor was a blessing in disguise; we found a better one." |
| throw someone under the bus | blame someone unfairly, often to protect yourself | "Don't throw the on-call engineer under the bus in the postmortem." (negative; useful to recognise, rarely to use) |
| a can of worms | a situation that creates many new problems once started | "Refactoring auth now would open a can of worms before the launch." |
| cut corners | do something poorly/cheaply to save time or money | "We cut corners on testing and paid for it later." |
| a fire drill | urgent, disruptive, often unnecessary emergency response | "Every release turns into a fire drill." |
| firefighting | reacting to urgent problems instead of planning | "We spend too much time firefighting and not enough on prevention." |
| put out fires | resolve urgent problems reactively | see above |

### 5. Negotiation and decisions (see also [negotiation & saying no](negotiation-and-saying-no.md))

| Expression | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| meet (someone) halfway | compromise | "We can meet halfway on scope if you extend the deadline." |
| off the table | not available as an option | "A budget increase is off the table this quarter." |
| on the table | available for discussion | "Everything is on the table except headcount." |
| a deal-breaker | a condition that would end negotiation if not met | "Data residency in the EU is a deal-breaker for this client." |
| hold out (for something) | refuse to agree until you get what you want | "They're holding out for a longer contract." |
| give in / cave | stop resisting and agree | "We shouldn't give in on the SLA terms." |
| back down | withdraw a position | "The vendor backed down on the price increase." |
| drive a hard bargain | negotiate firmly for good terms | "Procurement drives a hard bargain." |
| a win-win | an outcome good for both sides | "Extending the timeline for a lower price is a win-win." |
| sweeten the deal | add extra value to make an offer more attractive | "They sweetened the deal with a free support tier." |
| a sticking point | the specific issue blocking agreement | "Liability caps are the sticking point." |
| the ball is in your court | it's your turn to decide/act | "We've sent our final offer; the ball is in their court." |

### 6. Teamwork and leadership

| Expression | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| pull your weight | do your fair share of the work | "Everyone needs to pull their weight before the release." (can sound critical; use carefully) |
| have someone's back | support and protect a colleague | "As the lead, I've got your back if this goes wrong." |
| go the extra mile | do more than expected | "She went the extra mile to help the client migrate." |
| step up | take responsibility, especially in a gap | "He stepped up when the lead was on leave." |
| on the same team | aligned in goals, not adversarial | "We're on the same team here; let's solve this together." |
| throw your weight around | use authority in a heavy-handed, unwelcome way | negative; recognise, avoid using it about a senior colleague directly |
| lead from the front | lead by example, visibly and actively | "She leads from the front during incidents." |

### 7. Money and value

| Expression | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| the bottom line | the final, most important point (often financial) | "The bottom line is we need two more engineers." |
| cost a fortune / an arm and a leg | be very expensive (informal) | "That licence costs an arm and a leg." |
| break even | reach the point where costs equal revenue | "We'll break even on this tool in eight months." |
| cut costs | reduce expenses | |
| a shoestring budget | a very small budget | "We built the prototype on a shoestring budget." |
| bang for your buck | good value for money (informal, US) | "This vendor gives us more bang for our buck." |
| in the red / in the black | losing money / making a profit | |

### Register and safety notes
- **Safe everywhere, low risk of confusion:** *behind schedule, on track, low-hanging fruit, the big picture, a red flag, a win-win, the bottom line, meet halfway.*
- **Common but colourful - use with people who know English well:** *boil the ocean, dead in the water, a can of worms, throw under the bus, a ticking time bomb.*
- **Avoid in writing that will be translated or read by non-native speakers under time pressure:** most of these; prefer the plain equivalent (see [business vocabulary](business-tech-vocabulary.md) for plain terms like *risk, obstacle, delay, compromise*).
- **Never in customer-facing incident comms:** idioms read as flippant when something has gone wrong ("we're firefighting a can of worms" - avoid entirely).
- **US-specific:** *bang for your buck, a rain check, drive a hard bargain* (used in UK too, but of US origin).
- **UK-specific/more common there:** *a shoestring budget*, "brilliant", "spot on" (agreement, informal UK).

## Hands-on lab (60 min)
1. **Situational cards.** Pick five situations from your week (a status update, a scope negotiation, a risk callout). For each, choose one idiom from the matching section and write a real sentence.
2. **Register sort.** Take 15 idioms above and sort them into "safe everywhere / use with care / avoid with non-native or customer audiences".
3. **Plain-equivalent drill.** For 10 idioms, write the plain-English equivalent (as you'd use it with a global, less-fluent audience).
4. **Listen and log.** In your next three meetings, note every idiom a native speaker uses; check it against this list or a dictionary; add new ones you hear.
5. **Speak it.** Record 60 seconds narrating a project update using at least five idioms naturally (not forced), then a second version with zero idioms for a global audience.

## Questions

### L1 - Recall

??? question "Q1. What does \"low-hanging fruit\" mean, and give a workplace example."
    ??? success "Answer"
        An easy task or win, achievable with little effort. "Before the big refactor, let's grab the low-hanging fruit like fixing the flaky test."

??? question "Q2. Distinguish \"on the table\" and \"off the table\"."
    ??? success "Answer"
        *On the table* = available for discussion/negotiation. *Off the table* = not available, ruled out. "A discount is on the table; a longer contract is off the table."

??? question "Q3. What does \"throw someone under the bus\" mean, and is it appropriate to use about a colleague in a meeting?"
    ??? success "Answer"
        To blame someone unfairly, often to protect yourself. It's informal and carries negative judgement; appropriate to recognise the behavior, but risky to use directly about a specific colleague in their presence - it can escalate conflict.

??? question "Q4. Name three idioms that are safe for a global, less-fluent audience and three that are not."
    ??? success "Answer"
        Safe: behind schedule, the bottom line, a red flag. Riskier: boil the ocean, a can of worms, dead in the water (colourful, less predictable, easy to misread literally).

### L2 - Apply

??? question "Q5. Fill the gap with the right expression: (a) \"We're still ___ on the vendor contract; no decision yet.\" (b) \"Let's ___ after the demo to discuss next steps.\" (c) \"This bug is just ___; the real issue is the whole caching layer.\""
    ??? success "Answer"
        (a) up in the air; (b) touch base; (c) the tip of the iceberg.

??? question "Q6. Rewrite in plain English (no idioms) for a global audience: \"Let's not boil the ocean here - grab the low-hanging fruit first and circle back on the rest.\""
    ??? success "Answer"
        "Let's not try to solve everything at once. Let's fix the easy items first and revisit the rest later."

??? question "Q7. Choose the best idiom: \"Losing that customer was painful, but it forced us to fix our onboarding, so it turned out to be a ___.\""
    ??? success "Answer"
        "blessing in disguise" (something bad that led to a good outcome).

??? question "Q8. Match register to expression: formal-avoid, neutral-safe, informal-colourful for: \"behind schedule\", \"cost an arm and a leg\", \"a ticking time bomb\"."
    ??? success "Answer"
        "behind schedule" - neutral-safe (use in any report). "cost an arm and a leg" - informal-colourful (fine in speech, avoid in a formal doc; use "very expensive"). "a ticking time bomb" - informal-colourful, strong; use sparingly, mainly in speech.

### L3 - Judge and choose

??? question "Q9. In a postmortem you want to say the team reacted well under pressure without sounding like they were disorganised. Choose between \"firefighting\", \"put out fires\" and a plain phrase."
    ??? success "Answer"
        Both "firefighting" and "put out fires" can sound like there was no process (reactive, chaotic), which undercuts the message that the team did well. Prefer: "The team responded quickly and effectively to a fast-moving incident." Save "firefighting" for when you want to flag a *pattern* that needs fixing (too much unplanned reactive work).

??? question "Q10. A VP says, \"That's an interesting approach, but let's not move the goalposts here.\" What is actually being communicated?"
    ??? success "Answer"
        Polite pushback: the VP is signalling that the current requirements should not change mid-project, likely because a change was just proposed. It's a caution, not agreement - listen for the underlying concern (scope creep, fairness to the team) rather than the words alone.

??? question "Q11. Is it ever appropriate to avoid idioms altogether, even if you know them well?"
    ??? success "Answer"
        Yes: customer-facing incident communication, legal/contractual text, messages that will be machine-translated, and communication with colleagues who are clearly less fluent or new to the culture. In those contexts, plain, literal language reduces misunderstanding risk, even at the cost of sounding slightly less "native".

??? question "Q12. \"We need to sweeten the deal\" vs \"We need to offer more value\": which fits a negotiation debrief with your own team, and which fits an email summary to the other party's lawyer?"
    ??? success "Answer"
        "Sweeten the deal" is fine and natural internally with your team. "Offer more value" (or a specific concrete offer) is safer and clearer for external, formal, or legally reviewed communication, where idioms can be ambiguous or seem unprofessional.

### L4 - Real-world decisions

??? question "Q13. You are leading a globally distributed standup with colleagues from India, Germany, Brazil and the US. How do you calibrate idiom use, and what do you do if someone looks confused?"
    ??? success "Answer"
        Default to plain, safe idioms only (*behind schedule, on track, the bottom line*) and avoid colourful ones (*boil the ocean, a can of worms*). If someone looks confused, pause and rephrase immediately in plain words without singling them out: "To put it plainly - we're not going to try to fix everything at once." Over time, build a shared glossary of idioms the team has agreed to use, so newcomers can look them up.

??? question "Q14. You're asked to write the customer-facing summary of an incident where your team \"put out a fire\" caused by cutting corners on testing. Draft the key sentence without any idiom, but keep the honesty."
    ??? success "Answer"
        "The incident was caused by a change that did not go through our full testing process. We have restored service and are adding a mandatory test gate to prevent a repeat." No idioms, no blame language, ownership retained, and a concrete corrective action - see also [word nuance & register](word-nuance-register.md) for register calibration.

??? question "Q15. In a Staff-level interview, you want to sound fluent and natural without overusing idioms (which can seem like you're performing fluency). What's a good balance?"
    ??? success "Answer"
        Use two or three well-chosen, safe idioms naturally where they fit the story (*hit the ground running, the bottom line, a step in the right direction*), and rely mostly on precise vocabulary and clear structure for the substance. Avoid stacking idioms in one answer; interviewers judge clarity of thought more than idiom density, and forced idiom use can distract from your content.

## Real-world use cases
- **Status updates:** *on track, behind schedule, in the pipeline, a moving target*.
- **Scope negotiation with stakeholders:** *off the table, meet halfway, a deal-breaker, a sticking point*.
- **Risk callouts in design reviews:** *a red flag, a ticking time bomb, the elephant in the room* (used carefully).
- **1:1s and feedback:** *pull your weight* (careful), *go the extra mile, step up, have your back*.
- **All-hands/strategy talks:** *the big picture, a north star, raise the bar, low-hanging fruit*.

## Pitfalls & anti-patterns
- Using colourful idioms in customer-facing or legally reviewed text.
- Stacking multiple idioms in one sentence ("Let's not boil the ocean, but let's grab the low-hanging fruit and circle back if there's a can of worms").
- Using an idiom you've only seen in a textbook, not heard used recently.
- Misremembering the idiom's form ("push the needle" instead of "move the needle").
- Using negative-connotation idioms (*throw under the bus, cut corners*) about a specific named colleague.
- Assuming an idiom translates or is understood by every reader on a global team.
- Overusing "circle back" and "touch base" until they sound like filler.
